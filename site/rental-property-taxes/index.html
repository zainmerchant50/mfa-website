Schedule E or Schedule C: which form does my rental go on?

How you rent decides the form, whether self-employment tax applies and what can limit a loss (IRS Topic 415 and Publication 527).

How you rentFormSelf-employment taxWhat to watch
Long-term rental (tenant with a lease)Schedule ENoLoss up to $25,000 against other income if you actively participate; phases out from $100,000 to $150,000 of MAGI
Short-term rental, space onlySchedule ENoPassive loss rules; short average stays can change how a loss is treated
Short-term rental with hotel-style services (cleaning during stays, linens, meals)Schedule CUsually yesTreated like a business, not a rental
Your home, rented fewer than 15 daysNot reportedNoNo rental expenses deducted either
Your home, rented 15 days or moreSchedule E or COnly on Schedule CExpenses split by days and limited to rental income

Rental property tax questions

On Schedule E (Form 1040), which carries to your Form 1040. If you provide substantial services mainly for your guests' convenience, such as regular cleaning, changing linen or maid service, the IRS says to report the income and expenses on Schedule C instead. That is common for short-term rentals with hotel-style service.

Ordinary rental costs such as mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, repairs, maintenance and management fees. Improvements are different: they are capitalized and depreciated instead of deducted in one year. The IRS advises keeping repairs and improvements separate in your records.

Residential rental property is depreciated over 27.5 years, starting when it is ready and available for rent, even before the first tenant moves in. Land can't be depreciated, so only the building's share of your cost counts.

Generally an improvement, depreciated over 27.5 years. IRS Publication 527 lists a new roof, a furnace, central air and kitchen modernization as improvements. Three safe harbors can let you deduct more in the year you pay, including the $2,500 per item de minimis safe harbor. Our repairs vs improvements guide explains all three.

No. If you use a dwelling unit as a home and rent it for fewer than 15 days in the year, you don't report the rental income and you don't deduct the rental expenses.

When your personal use is more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days you rent it at a fair price. Then your rental deductions are limited, so a vacation home you also use needs a careful split of days and expenses.

Sometimes, up to $25,000 a year. If you actively participate in the rental, you may deduct up to $25,000 of rental loss from other income. The allowance shrinks once your modified AGI passes $100,000 and is generally gone at $150,000 ($50,000 and $75,000 if married filing separately). Losses above that carry forward.

Advance rent is taxable in the year you receive it. A security deposit you plan to return isn't income when you get it; if you keep part of it, or apply it as final rent, that part is income then.

Yes. New York starts from your federal adjusted gross income, so your federal Schedule E figures generally carry over. New Jersey treats net rental income as its own category and has its own depreciation rules, so some federal amounts need a state adjustment.

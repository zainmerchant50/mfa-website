Is It Safe to Use AI to Do My Taxes? Risks and Safe Steps (2026)

More people are asking chatbots to explain tax rules, sort receipts or even fill in their returns. The IRS and the Taxpayer Advocate have been clear about where that goes wrong. Here's what AI is good for at tax time, what it gets wrong, and how to use it without putting your money or your identity at risk.

By Zain Merchant, ACCANovember 24, 20264 min read
SharePost on LinkedIn

Executive summary

  • Accuracy: the Taxpayer Advocate says taxpayers should not rely on AI-generated answers to complex tax questions, and cites a review that found two tax chatbots gave inaccurate or irrelevant answers up to 50% of the time.
  • Responsibility: you are ultimately responsible for what's on your return, whoever or whatever helped prepare it.
  • Privacy: a public chatbot is not the place for your Social Security number, bank details or full tax documents.
  • Scams: the IRS's 2026 Dirty Dozen list includes AI-enabled phone calls that impersonate the IRS, and viral "tax hacks" that push people to file false returns.

Bottom line: Use AI to understand and organize, not to decide or file. Check every rule and number against irs.gov or a person who signs their work.

Is it safe to use AI to do my taxes? Partly. AI is a fast way to understand a form, build a document checklist or sort a pile of receipts. It is not a safe way to decide what goes on your return or to file it unchecked. Here's what the IRS and its Taxpayer Advocate say, the four risks that matter, and seven steps to use AI without regretting it.

AI tax advice accuracy: what does the IRS say?

Three IRS sources speak to it directly:

  • The Taxpayer Advocate Service (an independent office inside the IRS) says AI "may not be able to provide accurate answers to your complex tax questions," that taxpayers should not rely on AI-generated responses to complex tax questions, and that taxpayers are ultimately responsible for the information on their returns. It advises reviewing everything, verifying calculations and getting help from qualified professionals if needed.
  • The 2026 Dirty Dozen list (IR-2026-30) repeats that taxpayers should verify any calculations or information provided by artificial intelligence, in its warning about AI-enabled phone scams.
  • Guidance for tax professionals (IRS Office of Professional Responsibility, June 2026) says practitioners cannot rely solely on AI, must carefully review anything it drafts, and must handle client data only in secure, enterprise-approved AI tools. We cover that in AI in accounting and tax: what the IRS says in 2026.

What AI is good for at tax time

  • Explaining a form or a term in plain English ("What is Schedule C?").
  • Building a checklist of documents to gather before you file.
  • Sorting your own receipts into categories, with names and numbers removed.
  • Writing a list of questions for your preparer.

For bookkeeping, tools like ChatGPT for Accounting now draft entries and reconciliations inside QuickBooks and Xero for a person to review. The same rule applies: it drafts, you approve.

7 steps to use AI safely for your taxes

  1. Ask, don't hand over. Describe your situation in general terms. Leave out your name, Social Security number, address and account numbers.
  2. Name the year and state. Say "for the 2026 tax year, federal and New Jersey," then still check the answer.
  3. Ask for the source. Request the IRS form, publication or page, and open it yourself.
  4. Redo the math. Recalculate every number from your W-2s, 1099s and receipts.
  5. Don't file what you haven't reviewed. You're responsible for every line, and you should never sign a blank or incomplete return.
  6. Check who's preparing it. A paid preparer must sign the return and include a PTIN. A preparer who won't is a red flag, the IRS says.
  7. Ignore AI-voiced "IRS" calls and texts. Don't click links or open attachments from unexpected messages; send suspicious IRS emails to phishing@irs.gov.

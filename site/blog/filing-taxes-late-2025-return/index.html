Filing Taxes Late: Missed Oct. 15? What to Do Now for Your 2025 Return

Missed the extension deadline, or never filed for 2025? Here's what the penalties really cost, the one date still worth beating, and the order to do things in.

By Zain Merchant, ACCANovember 4, 20267 min read
SharePost on LinkedIn

The short version

  • File now, even if you can't pay. The late-filing penalty is 5% of the unpaid tax a month. The late-payment penalty is 0.5%. Filing stops the bigger one.
  • Extended to Oct. 15? File by Monday, Dec. 14, 2026. After 60 days late, a minimum penalty of $525 (or 100% of the tax, if less) kicks in.
  • Owed a refund? There's usually no late-filing penalty, but you only have 3 years from the due date to claim it.
  • New for 2025 returns: if you filed and paid on time for the 3 prior years, the IRS's automatic penalty relief may remove the penalties without you asking. Interest still applies.

Bottom line: Filing taxes late costs less than most people fear and more than it needs to if you keep waiting. File first, then deal with the balance.

If you missed the Oct. 15 extension deadline for your 2025 return, or never filed at all, the IRS hasn't forgotten about it, but this is very fixable. This guide to filing taxes late explains what the penalties actually cost, the one date that still matters this year, and what to do first.

What happens if you file taxes late?

A late tax return penalty comes in two parts, plus interest. Both are based on the tax you still owe, not on your income.

ChargeRateCap
Failure-to-file penalty5% of the unpaid tax for each month or part of a month the return is late25%
Minimum late-filing penalty (more than 60 days late)$525 or 100% of the tax owed, whichever is less (returns due in 2026)n/a
Failure-to-pay penalty0.5% of the unpaid tax a month (0.25% on an approved payment plan if you filed on time; 1% if you ignore a notice of intent to levy for 10 days)25%
Interest7% a year, compounded daily (Oct. 1 to Dec. 31, 2026). The IRS also charges interest on penalties.None

When both penalties apply in the same month, the late-filing penalty is reduced by the late-payment penalty, so the combined charge is 5% a month. After 5 months the late-filing penalty maxes out, but the late-payment penalty and interest keep running until the balance is paid.

A part of a month counts as a full month. Filing on Nov. 16 instead of Nov. 14 can add a whole month of penalty.

Why Dec. 14 matters if you filed an extension

The late-filing penalty runs from your due date including extensions. If you filed Form 4868 in April, your 2025 return was due Oct. 15, 2026. The tax law sets a minimum penalty for returns filed more than 60 days after that date, and 60 days after Oct. 15 is Monday, Dec. 14, 2026.

The minimum hits small balances hardest. If you owe $500 and file after Dec. 14, the late-filing penalty can be the full $500, because the minimum is the lesser of $525 or 100% of the tax. File by Dec. 14 and it would be a small fraction of that.

Remember that the extension only gave you more time to file. Your payment was still due April 15, 2026, so late-payment penalties and interest have been building since then.

If you didn't file an extension

Your 2025 return was due April 15, 2026. By now the late-filing penalty has reached its cap and the minimum already applies. What you can still control is the late-payment penalty and interest. Both grow every month you wait, so paying sooner still saves money.

A rough example

Say you extended, owe $3,000, and file and pay on Dec. 1, 2026. That's 2 months late on filing (4.5% a month after the reduction, about $270) and 8 months late on payment since April 15 (0.5% a month, about $120), plus interest. These numbers are illustrative and rounded. The IRS calculates the exact figures and sends a notice.

New for 2025 returns: automatic penalty relief

In July the IRS announced the Automatic Exemption from Penalty (AEP), which replaces the old First Time Abate request process (IR-2026-83). It applies to eligible original returns starting with tax year 2025.

  • Who qualifies: taxpayers who filed on time and paid any tax due in the 3 prior years.
  • What it covers: failure-to-file, failure-to-pay and failure-to-deposit penalties are not assessed when the return is processed.
  • What you do: nothing. If you qualify, the IRS sends a notice saying the relief was granted.
  • What it doesn't cover: the tax itself and the interest. You still owe both.

The IRS is still phasing the program in, so some people who qualify may get a penalty notice for a 2025 return anyway. If that happens, contact the IRS and ask for First Time Abate. If you don't have a clean 3-year history, you can still ask for relief for reasonable cause, such as a serious illness or a disaster, with documents to back it up.

If you can't pay the full balance

File anyway, pay what you can, and set up an IRS payment plan for the rest. The IRS online options, based on combined tax, penalties and interest:

PlanWho can apply onlineIRS setup fee online
Short-term (180 days or less)Owe less than $100,000$0 (individuals)
Long-term, automatic monthly debitOwe $50,000 or less and filed all required returns$29
Long-term, other payment methodsSame as above$69
Long-term, lower incomeSame as above$43, may be reimbursed

Interest and some penalties continue on a plan until you've paid in full. The reduced 0.25% late-payment rate applies only if your return was filed on time, which is one more reason not to wait.

If you're owed a refund

The late-filing penalty is a percentage of unpaid tax, so if your withholding or estimated payments cover your bill, there's usually nothing to charge. The catch is the deadline: you must file within 3 years of the return's due date to claim the refund, which for 2025 means April 2029. The IRS also holds refunds while any other year's return is past due.

Late filing checklist: what to do this week

  1. Gather your 2025 forms. W-2s, 1099s and last year's return. Missing one? Ask the employer or payer, or request the wage and income data with Form 4506-T (check the box on line 8).
  2. Check for disaster relief. Some areas hit by disasters have IRS deadline postponements running into early 2027. Look up your area on the IRS disaster relief page before you assume you're late.
  3. File the return. Extension filers: by Dec. 14 if you can.
  4. Pay what you can now and apply online for a payment plan for the rest.
  5. Self-employed? Unreported self-employment income doesn't earn Social Security credits until the return is filed. Our Schedule C guide for single-member LLCs covers what goes where.
  6. Don't forget your state. New Jersey charges its own late-filing penalty (below).

New Jersey late filing penalties

New Jersey's late-filing penalty is 5% of the tax due for each month or part of a month the return is late, up to 25%. The Division of Taxation may also charge $100 for each month the return is late, a late-payment penalty of 5% of the tax due, and interest at the prime rate plus 3%, compounded annually. So filing your NJ-1040 at the same time as your federal return matters too.

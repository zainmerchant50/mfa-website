Small Business Tax Write-Offs for 2026: What Counts, What's Limited and the Proof to Keep

A practical guide to what you can deduct for 2026, the limits that catch owners out (including two changes this year), and the paperwork that makes each write-off stick.

By Zain Merchant, ACCAOctober 30, 20267 min read
SharePost on LinkedIn

Executive summary

  • What counts: ordinary and necessary costs of running your business, and only the business share of anything you also use personally.
  • What's limited: business meals are 50%, entertainment is 0%, client gifts top out at $25 per person, and the simplified home office deduction is capped at $1,500.
  • Two 2026 changes: the business mileage rate rose midyear from 72.5 cents to 76 cents a mile on July 1, and snacks and meals you provide staff at the office are no longer deductible.
  • Proof: every write-off needs a document showing who you paid, how much, when, and what it was for. Keep records at least 3 years (4 for payroll).

Bottom line: Most write-offs are lost to missing paperwork or personal use, not to obscure rules. Check any expense in our Is It Deductible? tool before Dec. 31.

"Can I write that off?" is the question we hear most in the last two months of the year. This guide to small business tax write-offs for 2026 covers what generally counts, the limits that trip owners up, and the proof the IRS expects for each one. If you want a quick yes or no on a specific expense, look it up in our Is It Deductible? tool, which covers 59 common expenses with the 2026 rule and the record to keep for each.

What can I write off as a small business owner?

A business expense is deductible when it is ordinary (common and accepted in your line of work) and necessary (helpful and appropriate for the business; it doesn't have to be required). Three rules narrow that down:

  • Personal, living and family costs never count, even if you pay them from the business account.
  • Mixed-use items count only for the business share. If your phone is 60% business, 60% of the bill is the write-off.
  • Long-lived items are assets, not expenses. Equipment and furniture are capitalized and then deducted through depreciation, Section 179 or the small-purchase rule below.

What's limited or changed for 2026

The mileage rate changed midyear

The IRS set the 2026 business standard mileage rate at 72.5 cents a mile in Notice 2026-10, then raised it to 76 cents for driving on or after July 1, 2026 (Announcement 2026-11). Your log needs to split miles before and after July 1, so total them by half-year. If you own the car, you must choose the standard rate in the first year you use it for business to keep that option in later years.

Meals: 50% for clients, 0% for in-office staff meals

Business meals with clients, customers or prospects are still 50% deductible. What changed is food you provide employees at work: for amounts paid after 2025, coffee, snacks and meals provided through an on-site eating facility, as small (de minimis) perks, or for the employer's convenience are no longer deductible, apart from a few narrow exceptions in the law. Code these to their own account so they don't sit in "Meals" and get claimed at 50% by mistake.

Equipment: the generous rules are permanent

100% bonus depreciation is now permanent for qualifying property acquired after Jan. 19, 2025, and the Section 179 limit is $2,560,000 for tax years beginning in 2026, phased out above $4,090,000 of purchases. The asset must be placed in service (set up and ready to use) by Dec. 31, 2026. For New Jersey small businesses, the state doesn't follow federal bonus depreciation and caps Section 179 far lower, so your NJ deduction will differ. Our Section 179 vs. bonus depreciation guide walks through which to use and the New Jersey numbers.

Home office: an income limit, too

Beyond the $1,500 cap on the simplified method, neither method can create a loss from the home office: the deduction can't exceed the income from the business use of the home minus your other business expenses. W-2 employees can't claim it.

The proof to keep for each write-off

The IRS says your documents should show the payee, the amount paid, proof of payment, the date and a description that shows the cost was for the business. A card statement alone often doesn't show what you bought, so pair it with the receipt or invoice.

  • Everyday expenses: invoice or receipt plus the bank or card record that paid it.
  • Travel, meals, gifts and car: the amount, time, place and business purpose. For meals and gifts, note the business relationship of the person.
  • Mileage: a log kept as you go, not rebuilt in April, with odometer readings at the start and end of the year.
  • Equipment: when and how you acquired it, cost, Section 179 or depreciation claimed, how it was used, and later how and when you sold it.
  • Contractors: a Form W-9 collected before the first payment.

Common write-off mistakes we see at year-end

  1. Deducting the commute. Driving from home to your regular place of work is personal, however far it is.
  2. Writing off the whole phone or internet bill when part of it is personal.
  3. Lumping entertainment into meals. Tickets and outings are 0%, not 50%.
  4. Booking equipment as "office expense." Record it as a fixed asset so your books, depreciation schedule and return agree.
  5. Paying personal bills from the business account. It doesn't make them deductible, and it makes the books harder to defend. Our Schedule C guide for single-member LLCs shows where each cost belongs on the return.

Tax deductions for artists

Tax savings for artists start with what you can deduct.

Business expenses come off your art income before income tax and self-employment tax are figured. They need to be ordinary (common for artists) and necessary (helpful for your art business), and you need the records to back them up.

  • Art supplies and materialsPaint, canvas, paper, clay, film, strings. Supplies go on Schedule C line 22; materials that become pieces you sell are counted in cost of goods sold instead.
  • Studio rent or a home studioStudio rent is a business expense. A home studio counts only if that space is used regularly and exclusively for your art business; the simplified method allows $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet.
  • Equipment and softwareCameras, computers, instruments, kilns and design software are usually depreciated, but Section 179 or bonus depreciation can let you deduct them in the first year.
  • Art-fair booth fees and marketingBooth and jury fees, your website, ads, business cards and portfolio printing are costs of selling your work.
  • Shipping and packagingPostage, boxes, tubes and packing materials for the orders you ship.
  • Platform and payment feesMarketplace listing and transaction fees, payment-processing fees and commissions galleries keep.
  • Business mileageDriving to fairs, galleries, suppliers and clients, at the IRS standard mileage rate or your actual costs. Keep a log as you go.
  • Classes and workshopsDeductible when they maintain or improve skills you already use in your art business, not when they qualify you for a new line of work.

Not sure about an expense? Search it in Is It Deductible?, or work out a home studio with the home office calculator and your driving with the mileage calculator.

Common questions

Artist tax questions, answered

It depends on whether you run it to make a profit. The IRS weighs nine factors, including whether you keep complete books and records, the time and effort you put in, whether you change methods to improve profitability, and whether you've made a profit in some years. No single factor decides it. Either way, the income has to be reported. Running it as a business is what lets you deduct your expenses on Schedule C. Hobby expenses can't be deducted at all, and a 2025 tax law made that permanent.

Expenses that are ordinary (common and accepted in your field) and necessary (helpful and appropriate for your business): supplies and materials, studio rent or a home studio, equipment and software, booth fees and marketing, shipping, platform and payment fees, business mileage and classes that improve the skills you already use. Keep receipts and a mileage log. See the list of tax deductions for artists on this page.

Those aren't income, so you report your real sales and then deduct fees, shipping and your other costs on Schedule C. Payment apps and marketplaces send a 1099-K when your payments top $20,000 and 200 transactions. Card payments have no minimum, and some states set lower limits. Income is taxable whether or not you get a form.

Usually, if you expect to owe $1,000 or more when you file, because no employer is withholding tax from your art income. Most people avoid the underpayment penalty by paying at least 90% of this year's tax or 100% of the tax on last year's return, whichever is smaller. Payments are figured with Form 1040-ES across four payment periods a year. Our quarterly estimated tax calculator shows the numbers, and our quarterly estimated tax service is $199 for the year.

Not for taxes. By default the IRS ignores a single-member LLC for income tax (a "disregarded entity"), so you still report your art income on Schedule C with your Form 1040 and still pay self-employment tax. An LLC can elect to be taxed as a corporation, which only makes sense once profits are steady; we run the numbers first. What an LLC does add is a separate legal business for contracts, bank accounts and state filings, with state fees that vary by state. Questions about personal liability are for an attorney.

Sales tax is set by each state, not the IRS, and rules, rates and temporary-vendor permits differ by state and sometimes by city. Selling in person can mean registering with that state and collecting its sales tax, and some events ask for your permit number. Check the revenue department for each state where you sell. Selling in New Jersey? Read our NJ sales tax guide. Sales tax you collect from buyers and pass on to the state isn't your income.

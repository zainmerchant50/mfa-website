Booth renter tax questions

Usually yes. If you rent your chair or suite, keep your own clients and set your own prices and hours, you are generally running your own business. You report your income and expenses on Schedule C and pay self-employment tax on your profit.

15.3% of your net earnings from self-employment: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. It usually applies once your net earnings are $400 or more, and it's figured on Schedule SE. Half of it is deductible when you work out your income tax.

Yes. Rent you pay for the chair, booth or suite you work from is a business expense on Schedule C, along with products, tools, license renewals, education, booking software and the business share of your phone.

Yes. Tips are income whether they come in cash, by card or through an app. When you're self-employed they're part of your business income on Schedule C. Keep a daily record so your numbers match what your payment apps report.

It can. From 2025 through 2028, employees and self-employed people may deduct qualified tips, up to $25,000 a year, whether or not they itemize. Barbers, hairdressers, hairstylists, cosmetologists, manicurists, makeup artists, skincare specialists and eyebrow and eyelash technicians are on the IRS list of tipped occupations. The deduction phases out above $150,000 of modified AGI ($300,000 joint), and some self-employed businesses are excluded, so we check that you qualify.

Most booth renters do, because no employer withholds tax from what clients pay you. Federal estimates for 2026 income are due April 15, June 15 and September 15, 2026 and January 15, 2027. See quarterly estimated taxes.

You may get a Form 1099-K from a payment app or card processor, and a 1099-NEC from a salon that pays you $2,000 or more for work in 2026. Either way, all your income is taxable, including cash, so we match every form to your records.

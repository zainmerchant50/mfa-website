Who it's for

F-1, J-1 and H-1B tax returns: who we help.

The right forms depend on your visa, how long you've been in the US and what you earned, not on where you're from.

F-1 and J-1 students

Including OPT and CPT. In your first US years you are usually a nonresident for tax, which means Form 1040-NR and Form 8843.

J-1 scholars, researchers and teachers

Teachers and trainees follow a different exempt-years rule than students, so we check your years in the US carefully.

H-1B and other work visas

Most H-1B workers pass the substantial presence test and file Form 1040. A first or last year in the US can be dual-status (part nonresident, part resident).

No US income this year

Students and scholars who were in the US but earned nothing usually still need to send Form 8843.

What you'll need

What you'll need for Form 1040-NR and Form 8843.

Missing something? Send what you have and we'll tell you what else to get.

  • Passport and visa detailsThe visa type and the dates on it.
  • Form I-20 or Form DS-2019I-20 for F-1 students, DS-2019 for J-1 students and scholars.
  • Income formsW-2 for wages, 1042-S for scholarships, fellowships or treaty-exempt pay, and any 1099s.
  • SSN or ITINIf you have one.
  • US entry and exit datesFor this year and earlier years. Your I-94 travel history is the easiest record.

Usually yes, but only Form 8843. If you were in the US as an F, J, M or Q student, or a J or Q teacher or trainee, and you don't count those days toward the substantial presence test, the IRS requires Form 8843 to explain why. With no tax return to file, you mail Form 8843 on its own by the Form 1040-NR due date. If you file late, the IRS can count those days, which can change your tax status. It depends on the substantial presence test: at least 31 days in the US this year and 183 days over three years, counting all of this year's days, one-third of last year's and one-sixth of the year before. Days as an exempt individual don't count. Students on F, J, M or Q visas are generally exempt for up to five calendar years; J and Q teachers and trainees lose the exemption if they were exempt in two of the six years before. H-1B workers count every day, so most become residents and file Form 1040. Tax residency is separate from immigration status.

    Home and workspace

    6

    Home office

    Partly

    Partly

    If a specific spot in your home is used only and regularly for your business, and it's your main place of work (or where you meet clients), part of your home costs counts. The easy way: $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet.

    For example: Priya, a bookkeeper, works in a 150-square-foot spare room she uses only for work. With the easy method, that's 150 × $5 = $750.

    What to keep as proof

    • Measurements of your office and your whole home
    • Photos showing the space is only used for work
    • Your home bills and receipts, if you use the regular method

    Common mistake

    Calling a corner of the guest room or the kitchen table an office. The space has to be used only for work, and regularly.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Depends on the facts
    The rule
    You can deduct part of your home costs if a specific area is used regularly and exclusively for your business and is your principal place of business (or where you meet clients). Use the simplified method ($5 per square foot) or the regular method (your business-use percentage of actual home costs, on Form 8829).
    Limits
    Simplified method: $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet, so $1,500 at most. Either method can't exceed the gross income from the business use of your home minus your other business expenses; under the regular method the excess carries over to the next year, under the simplified method it doesn't.
    Records
    Measurements or a floor plan of the office and the whole home, photos showing exclusive use, and the bills, receipts and canceled checks for every home expense you deduct (and your home's basis if you claim depreciation).
    Who it applies to
    Self-employed people. W-2 employees can't claim a home office deduction.

    Official sources

    Office rent

    Yes

    Yes

    Rent for an office, shop, studio or storefront you use for your business counts in full.

    For example: Lena rents a salon suite for $900 a month. All $10,800 for the year is a business expense.

    What to keep as proof

    • The signed lease
    • Monthly invoices or statements
    • Proof you paid

    Common mistake

    Writing off rent on your own home as office rent. Home costs only count through the home office rules.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Deductible
    The rule
    Rent you pay for office, retail or other business space is deductible in the year it applies to. Rent for vehicles, machinery and equipment is deductible too, for the business share of use.
    Limits
    No dollar limit. For rented vehicles, machinery and equipment, only the business share of use counts.
    Records
    The signed lease, monthly invoices or statements, and proof of payment.
    Who it applies to
    Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

    Official sources

    • IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
    • 26 CFR § 1.162-1, Business expenses

    Coworking space or shared desk

    Yes

    Yes

    A coworking membership, desk rental or meeting room you book for work counts as rent for business space. If the membership bundles in personal perks like a gym, only the business part counts.

    For example: Marcus, a consultant, pays $250 a month for a hot desk. That's $3,000 a year he can write off.

    What to keep as proof

    • Your membership agreement
    • Monthly invoices and proof you paid
    • A quick note of what you use the space for

    Common mistake

    Writing off the whole membership when it bundles in personal perks like a gym. Only the business part counts.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Deductible
    The rule
    A coworking membership, desk rental or meeting-room booking used for your business is rent for business space and is deductible.
    Limits
    Only the business share counts if part of the membership is for personal use (for example, a gym or social perks bundled in).
    Records
    Membership agreement, monthly invoices and proof of payment. A short note of what the space is used for helps.
    Who it applies to
    Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

    Official sources

    • IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
    • 26 CFR § 1.162-1, Business expenses

    Utilities (electric, gas, water)

    Partly

    Partly

    For a shop, office or studio, yes. For your home, only through the home office deduction: the work-space share of the bills counts if you use the regular method.

    For example: Sofia's bakery has its own electric bill, so all of it counts. Her home electric bill counts only for the share of her home that's a qualifying office.

    What to keep as proof

    • Utility bills and proof you paid
    • Your square-footage math, if it's for a home office

    Common mistake

    Writing off the whole home electric or gas bill. At home, only the office's share counts, and only with the regular home office method.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Depends on the facts
    The rule
    Utilities for a business location (an office, shop or studio) are deductible. Utilities for your home are deductible only through a home office deduction, as part of your business-use percentage under the regular method.
    Limits
    No dollar limit for a business location. At home, only the business-use share counts, and the home office income limit applies.
    Records
    Utility bills and proof of payment; for a home office, your square-footage calculation.
    Who it applies to
    Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

    Official sources

    • IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
    • IRS Publication 587 (2025), Business Use of Your Home

    Office supplies and postage

    Yes

    Yes

    Paper, ink, pens, stamps and other supplies you use up in your business count. Things that last well over a year are treated as equipment instead.

    For example: Jay, an Etsy seller, spends $40 a month on printer ink and paper. That's $480 a year in write-offs.

    What to keep as proof

    • Receipts showing what you bought and when

    Common mistake

    Lumping a printer or a desk in with supplies. Things that last well over a year are equipment, with their own rules.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Deductible
    The rule
    Office supplies and postage used in your business are deductible. Materials and supplies are generally deducted as you use them, and items you normally use up within a year can be deducted when bought.
    Limits
    Items that last substantially longer than a year are generally depreciated instead (or expensed under the de minimis safe harbor or Section 179).
    Records
    Receipts or invoices showing what you bought and when.
    Who it applies to
    Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

    Official sources

    • IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)

    Work clothes and uniforms

    Usually not

    Usually not

    Only if your work requires it and it isn't something you could wear as everyday clothing, like protective gear, scrubs or a uniform you wouldn't wear outside work. Regular clothes don't count, even if you only wear them for work.

    For example: An electrician's safety boots and protective gloves count. A stylist's black outfits for the salon don't, because they could be worn anywhere.

    What to keep as proof

    • Receipts
    • A note of why your work requires it (photos help)

    Common mistake

    Writing off clothes you could wear outside work, like a suit or black pants, even if you only wear them for work.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Depends on the facts
    The rule
    Clothing is deductible only if your work requires it and it isn't suitable for ordinary street wear, such as protective gear or a uniform you wouldn't wear outside work. Everyday clothes are personal, even if you only wear them for work.
    Limits
    No dollar limit. The test is objective: if the item could be worn as ordinary street clothing, it doesn't qualify, however you actually use it.
    Records
    Receipts, plus a note of why the item is required for your work and not suitable for everyday wear (photos help).
    Who it applies to
    Self-employed people.

    Official sources

    • IRS Federal, State and Local Governments directive: Tax treatment of uniforms (2015)
    • IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
    • 26 U.S.C. § 162, Trade or business expenses

    Car and travel

    5

    Car mileage (standard rate)

    Partly

    Partly

    Yes for business driving, never for your commute. The easy way: multiply your business miles by the IRS rate, 72.5 cents a mile for January to June 2026 and 76 cents from July to December. Business parking and tolls count on top.

    For example: Tom, a handyman, drives 4,000 business miles to clients and supply stores between January and June. That's 4,000 × $0.725 = $2,900.

    What to keep as proof

    • A mileage log kept as you go: date, where, why and how many miles
    • Your odometer reading at the start and end of the year

    Common mistake

    Counting the drive from home to your regular shop or office. That's commuting, and it never counts.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Partly deductible
    The rule
    Deduct your business miles times the IRS standard mileage rate instead of tracking actual car costs. Business miles are trips to clients, between work locations and for business errands, never the commute.
    Limits
    2026 business rate: 72.5 cents a mile for Jan. 1 to June 30 and 76 cents a mile for July 1 to Dec. 31 (2025: 70 cents). You must choose the standard rate in the first year you use a car you own for business; with five or more cars used at the same time you can't use it. Business parking and tolls are deductible on top.
    Records
    A mileage log kept as you go: date, destination, business purpose and miles for each trip, plus total miles for the year and odometer readings.
    Who it applies to
    Self-employed people who own or lease the car. W-2 employees generally can't deduct unreimbursed car costs.

    Official sources

    Car expenses (actual costs)

    Partly

    Partly

    Instead of the mileage rate, you can write off the work share of what your car really costs: gas, repairs, tires, insurance, registration, and depreciation or lease payments. If 70% of your miles are for work, 70% of those costs count.

    For example: Ana drove 10,000 miles this year, 7,000 of them for her cleaning business. With $6,000 in car costs, 70% counts: $4,200.

    What to keep as proof

    • Every car receipt: gas, repairs, insurance
    • Your purchase or lease papers
    • A mileage log with work miles and total miles

    Common mistake

    Writing off 100% of a car you also drive personally, with no mileage log to back up your work percentage.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Partly deductible
    The rule
    Instead of the standard rate, you can deduct the business share of what the car actually costs: gas, oil, repairs, tires, insurance, registration, licenses and depreciation or lease payments. If you also drive it personally, only the business-use percentage counts.
    Limits
    Business-use percentage = business miles divided by total miles. Depreciation is subject to the yearly limits for passenger cars; a heavy SUV's Section 179 deduction is capped at $32,000 for tax years beginning in 2026 ($31,300 in 2025).
    Records
    Every receipt for car costs, the purchase or lease papers, and a mileage log showing business and total miles.
    Who it applies to
    Self-employed people. W-2 employees generally can't deduct unreimbursed car costs.

    Official sources

    Parking and tolls

    Yes

    Yes

    Parking and tolls on business trips count, on top of mileage or actual car costs. Parking at your own regular workplace doesn't, because that's commuting.

    For example: Visiting a client downtown, Raj pays $18 to park and $6 in tolls. Both count.

    What to keep as proof

    • Receipts or toll statements
    • A matching note in your mileage log

    Writing off the monthly spot at your own office or shop. That's part of your commute.

    For your accountant

    Deductible
    The rule
    Parking fees and tolls for business trips are deductible on top of either the standard mileage rate or actual expenses.
    Limits
    Parking at your own regular place of business is a commuting cost and isn't deductible.
    Records
    Receipts or toll statements, matched to trips in your mileage log.
    Who it applies to
    Self-employed people; employees only in limited cases.

    Business travel (flights, hotels)

    Yes

    Yes

    Yes, when work takes you away from home overnight: flights, hotels, rides and taxis there, baggage and laundry. Meals on the trip count at half. A trip that's mainly a vacation doesn't count.

    For example: A photographer flies to Chicago for a three-day wedding shoot. Her flight, hotel and rides count, and her meals count at half.

    What to keep as proof

    • Hotel receipts and any receipt of $75 or more
    • The dates, where you went and the business reason

    Common mistake

    Writing off a family vacation because you took a few work calls. The trip has to be mainly for business.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Deductible
    The rule
    Ordinary and necessary costs of traveling away from your tax home overnight for business are deductible: transportation, lodging, taxis or rideshare at the destination, baggage, laundry and business calls. Meals on the trip are 50% deductible.
    Limits
    The trip has to take you away from your tax home (the general area of your main place of business) longer than a normal workday, needing sleep or rest. A trip that is mainly personal is a personal expense.
    Records
    Receipts for lodging and anything $75 or more, plus the dates, destination and business purpose of the trip.
    Who it applies to
    Self-employed people. W-2 employees generally can't deduct unreimbursed travel.

    Official sources

    • IRS Topic no. 511, Business travel expenses
    • IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses
    • 26 CFR § 1.274-5, Substantiation requirements

    Meals while traveling

    Partly

    PartlyCounts at half (50%)

    Meals while you're away overnight on business count at 50%. Keep your receipts, or use the IRS daily meal allowance for the area instead.

    For example: On a two-day trip to a trade show, Kim spends $120 on meals. She can write off $60.

    What to keep as proof

    • Meal receipts, or with the daily allowance, the dates, place and reason for the trip

    Common mistake

    Writing off 100% of meals on the road. They count at half.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Partly deductible
    The rule
    Meals while you're traveling away from home on business are generally 50% deductible. You can deduct actual costs or use the standard meal allowance for the area instead of keeping meal receipts.
    Limits
    50% of the cost (80% for workers subject to Department of Transportation hours-of-service limits, such as long-haul truck drivers).
    Records
    Receipts if you deduct actual costs; with the standard meal allowance, records of the time, place and business purpose of the trip.
    Who it applies to
    Self-employed people traveling overnight for business.

    Food and entertaining

    4

    Business meals

    Partly

    PartlyCounts at half (50%)

    A meal with a client, customer or business contact counts at 50%, tax and tip included, as long as you're there, it isn't over the top, and there's a real business reason.

    For example: Lisa, a realtor, takes a client to lunch for $60 with tip. She can write off $30.

    What to keep as proof

    • The receipt (a must for $75 or more)
    • Who you ate with, how you know them and what you talked about
    • Jot it down the same day

    Common mistake

    Keeping the receipt but not noting who you ate with and why. The receipt alone doesn't show the business reason.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Partly deductible
    The rule
    A meal with a client, customer or business contact is generally 50% deductible if it isn't lavish, you (or an employee) are there, and it has a business purpose.
    Limits
    50% of the cost, including tax and tip. Food and drinks at an entertainment event count only if bought separately or listed separately on the bill.
    Records
    Receipt (required for $75 or more), plus a note of the date, place, who attended, their business relationship to you and what you discussed. Write it down at or near the time.
    Who it applies to
    Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

    Entertainment (tickets, golf, shows)

    Usually not

    Usually not

    Game tickets, golf, concerts and shows don't count, even with a client and a real business reason. Food bought separately at the event can still count as a half-off business meal.

    For example: Taking a client to a ball game: the $200 tickets don't count. The $40 of food bought separately counts at half, so $20.

    What to keep as proof

    • Receipts for any food bought separately

    Common mistake

    Writing off game tickets or a round of golf because a client came along. Entertainment doesn't count, even with a real business reason.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Not deductible
    The rule
    Entertainment is not deductible, even with a clear business purpose. That covers tickets to games and shows, golf, and similar outings with clients or prospects.
    Limits
    0%. Food and drinks bought separately at an entertainment event, or listed separately on the bill, can still be a 50% business meal.
    Records
    Keep records anyway if a separately billed meal was involved.
    Who it applies to
    Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

    Official sources

    • IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses
    • 26 U.S.C. § 274, Disallowance of certain entertainment, etc., expenses

    Staff parties and team events

    Yes

    Yes

    Yes, in full. A holiday party or summer picnic mainly for your team counts 100%, not just half, as long as it's for your staff in general and not only your top earners.

    For example: A salon owner throws a $1,200 holiday dinner for her eight stylists. All of it counts.

    What to keep as proof

    • Invoices and receipts

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Deductible
    The rule
    Recreational and social events mainly for your employees, such as a holiday party or summer picnic, are an exception to the entertainment and 50% meal limits.
    Limits
    100%, as long as the event is mainly for employees in general rather than for highly compensated employees only.
    Records
    Invoices and receipts, the date, and a guest list showing it was for staff.
    Who it applies to
    Businesses with employees.

    Snacks and meals for employees at work

    Usually not

    Usually not

    No, starting in 2026. Snacks, coffee and meals you provide to employees at work are no longer deductible, apart from a few narrow exceptions. Staff parties follow a different rule.

    For example: The break-room coffee and snacks for your team don't count from 2026 on.

    What to keep as proof

    • Invoices, plus what the food was for, so the right rule is used

    Common mistake

    Still writing off office snacks and catered team lunches out of habit. That stopped counting in 2026.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Not deductible
    The rule
    Starting in 2026, an employer can no longer deduct the cost of food and drinks provided to employees through an on-site eating facility, as de minimis fringe benefits, or for the employer's convenience. There are narrow exceptions in the law.
    Limits
    0% for amounts paid or incurred after Dec. 31, 2025. Staff parties and other recreational events mainly for employees follow a different rule.
    Records
    Invoices, plus what the food was for, so the right rule can be applied.
    Who it applies to
    Businesses with employees.

    Official sources

    • IRS Publication 15-B (2026), Employer's Tax Guide to Fringe Benefits
    • 26 U.S.C. § 274, Disallowance of certain entertainment, etc., expenses

    Cell phone and phone service

    Partly

    Partly

    The work share of your phone bill counts. If you use your phone 60% for work, you can write off 60% of the bill. The phone itself is equipment and is written off separately.

    For example: Mia's phone bill is $80 a month and about 60% of her use is for her design clients. That's $48 a month, or $576 a year.

    What to keep as proof

    • Your phone bills
    • A sample month showing how much is work, to back up your percentage

    Common mistake

    Writing off 100% of a phone you also use for personal calls and apps.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Partly deductible
    The rule
    The business share of your cell phone plan is deductible. For a home landline, the basic charge for the first line into your home is personal, but business long-distance calls and a second line used only for business are deductible.
    Limits
    Only the business-use percentage of a mixed-use phone. The phone itself is equipment: deduct it under the de minimis safe harbor, Section 179 or depreciation.
    Records
    Phone bills, plus a reasonable basis for your business-use percentage (for example, a sample month of itemized usage).
    Who it applies to
    Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

    Official sources

    Yes

    Laptop or computer

    Yes

    Yes

    Usually yes, for the share you use for work. You can often write off a work laptop all at once (for example, if it costs $2,500 or less), or spread it over a few years.

    For example: Chris buys an $1,800 laptop and uses it 90% for his consulting work. He can write off 90%, or $1,620.

    What to keep as proof

    • The receipt
    • The date you started using it for work
    • A rough record of work versus personal use

    Common mistake

    Writing off 100% of a laptop the family also uses. Only the work share counts.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Depends on the facts
    The rule
    A computer used in your business is a business asset. You can deduct it all at once using the de minimis safe harbor (if $2,500 or less per item or invoice), Section 179 or 100% bonus depreciation, or depreciate it over several years. If you also use it personally, only the business share counts.
    Limits
    Since 2018 computers are no longer "listed property," but personal use still reduces the deduction. If business use later falls so it's no longer mainly for business, part of a Section 179 deduction can be recaptured.
    Records
    The receipt or invoice, the date you started using it for business, and a record of business versus personal use.
    Who it applies to
    Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

    Equipment and machinery (Section 179)

    Yes

    Yes

    Furniture, machines and other equipment for your business can usually be written off in full the year you start using it, instead of over several years. The write-off can't be more than your business income; any extra moves to next year.

    For example: A landscaper buys a $12,000 mower and starts using it in May. If his business income covers it, he can write off all $12,000 this year.

    What to keep as proof

    • Invoices
    • The date you started using it
    • How much you use it for work

    Common mistake

    Expecting it to create a loss. It can't be more than your business income; the extra moves to next year.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Deductible
    The rule
    Section 179 lets you deduct the cost of qualifying business equipment, furniture and off-the-shelf software in the year you start using it, instead of depreciating it over several years.
    Limits
    Tax years beginning in 2026: up to $2,560,000, reduced dollar for dollar once qualifying purchases exceed $4,090,000 (2025: $2,500,000 and $4,000,000). Heavy SUVs are capped at $32,000. The deduction can't exceed your taxable income from active business; the excess carries forward.
    Records
    Invoices, the placed-in-service date, business-use percentage, and Form 4562.
    Who it applies to
    Businesses of any type that buy qualifying property for active business use.

    Yes

    Yes

    Small equipment purchases ($2,500 or less)

    Yes

    Yes

    Items that cost $2,500 or less each (or per invoice) can be written off right away instead of over several years, if you choose this rule each year on your tax return.

    Repairs vs. improvements

    Yes

    Yes

    Fixing something to keep it working counts right away. Making it better, bigger or fit for a new use is an improvement, written off over several years. Smaller businesses may be able to write off repairs and improvements on a building right away, up to the lesser of $10,000 or 2% of the building's cost.

    For example: Patching a leak in the salon's roof: write it off now. Replacing the whole roof: that's an improvement, written off over time.

    What to keep as proof

    • Contractor invoices describing the work
    • Before-and-after photos

    Common mistake

    Writing off a full renovation as a "repair." If it makes the property better, bigger or fit for a new use, it's an improvement.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Depends on the facts
    The rule
    Incidental repairs and routine maintenance that keep property in normal working order are deductible. Work that betters, restores or adapts the property to a new use is an improvement: it's capitalized and depreciated.
    Limits
    Small taxpayer safe harbor: buildings with an unadjusted basis of $1 million or less, if your average gross receipts are $10 million or less, can deduct repairs, maintenance and improvements up to the lesser of $10,000 or 2% of the building's basis.
    Records
    Contractor invoices describing the work done, before-and-after photos, and any safe harbor election statements.
    Who it applies to
    Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

    Yes

    Yes

    Courses and learning

    1

    Courses, training and conferences

    Yes

    Yes

    Yes, if it keeps up or improves skills for the work you already do. Not if it trains you for a new line of work, or covers the basic requirements to get into your field.

    Interest on business loans and credit cards

    Partly

    Partly

    Interest on money borrowed for your business counts. On a card or loan used for both business and personal spending, only the business share counts.

    For example: Mo borrows $10,000 to buy a used van for his moving business. The interest on that loan counts. Interest on his personal shopping doesn't.

    What to keep as proof

    • Loan agreements and statements
    • Records of what the borrowed money paid for

    Common mistake

    Writing off all the interest on a card or loan you also use personally. Only the business share counts.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Partly deductible
    The rule
    Interest on money borrowed for your business is deductible. Interest is allocated by how the borrowed money was used, so on a card or loan used for both, only the business share counts. Personal interest isn't deductible.
    Limits
    Small businesses that meet the gross receipts test ($32 million average for tax years beginning in 2026; $31 million in 2025) are exempt from the business interest limit. Larger businesses may be limited.
    Records
    Loan agreements, Forms 1098 and statements, and records tracing how loan proceeds were spent.
    Who it applies to
    Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

    Unpaid invoices (bad debts)

    Usually not

    Usually not

    Usually not for small businesses. If you never counted the unpaid invoice as income (most small businesses don't until they're paid), there's nothing to write off.

    For example: A designer bills $1,500 and the client never pays. She never reported that $1,500 as income, so there's no deduction, and no tax on it either.

    What to keep as proof

    • The invoice and proof it was counted as income
    • Your attempts to collect

    Common mistake

    Writing off an unpaid invoice you never reported as income. There's nothing to deduct.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Depends on the facts
    The rule
    A business bad debt is deductible only if the amount was already included in your income. Most small businesses use the cash method, so an unpaid invoice was never income and can't be deducted.
    Limits
    Deduct it only in the year it becomes wholly or partly worthless, after reasonable collection efforts. Refund claims for bad debts have a 7-year window.
    Records
    The invoice, proof it was included in income, collection attempts and why it became worthless.
    Who it applies to
    Mostly accrual-method businesses, and loans or credit sales made in the business.

    Official sources

    Starting a business

    2

    Startup costs

    Partly

    Partly

    Costs before you open, like market research, pre-launch ads or training, can be written off up to $5,000 in your first year. The rest is spread over 15 years.

    For example: Before opening her bakery, Rosa spends $8,000 on research, ads and training. She writes off $5,000 in year one and the other $3,000 bit by bit over 15 years.

    What to keep as proof

    • Receipts dated before you opened
    • The date your business started

    Common mistake

    Writing off every pre-opening cost in year one. Only up to $5,000 counts right away.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Partly deductible
    The rule
    Costs of investigating or setting up a business before it opens (market research, pre-opening advertising, training, consultants) can be deducted up to $5,000 in the year you start, with the rest amortized over 180 months.
    Limits
    $5,000 deduction, reduced dollar for dollar once total startup costs exceed $50,000 (none at $55,000 or more). The remainder is deducted evenly over 180 months from the month the business starts.
    Records
    Receipts and invoices dated before opening, and the date the business began.
    Who it applies to
    Any new active trade or business.

    Official sources

    • 26 U.S.C. § 195, Start-up expenditures

    Partly

    Products and shipping

    Inventory and cost of goods sold

    Yes

    Yes

    Products you buy or make to sell count when they sell, not when you buy them. Smaller businesses can often use a simpler method.

    For example: A candle maker buys $2,000 of wax and jars and uses $1,500 of it in candles she sells this year. The $1,500 counts now; the rest counts when those candles sell.

    What to keep as proof

    • Purchase invoices
    • Inventory counts at the start and end of the year

    Common mistake

    Writing off a big stock order the day you buy it. Products generally count as they sell.

    Limits
    The small business option is available if average annual gross receipts for the prior 3 years are $32 million or less for tax years beginning in 2026 ($31 million for 2025).
    Records
    Purchase invoices, beginning and ending inventory counts, and your inventory method.
    Who it applies to
    Businesses that sell products.

    Taxes and licenses

    3

    Self-employment tax

    Partly

    PartlyCounts at half (50%)

    You can write off half of your self-employment tax on your personal return. It lowers your income tax, not the self-employment tax itself.

    For example: If your self-employment tax is $7,000, you can deduct $3,500.

    What to keep as proof

    • Schedule SE and your Schedule C (tax software or your preparer handles this)

    Common mistake

    Putting it on your business schedule. Half of it is deducted on your personal return instead.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Partly deductible
    The rule
    Half of your self-employment tax (the employer-equivalent part) is deductible in figuring adjusted gross income, on Schedule 1. It isn't a Schedule C expense and doesn't reduce the SE tax itself.
    Limits
    50% of SE tax. SE tax is 15.3% on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings, with the Social Security part stopping at the $184,500 wage base for 2026.
    Records
    Schedule SE and your Schedule C.
    Who it applies to
    Self-employed people who owe SE tax (net earnings of $400 or more).

    Business taxes

    Partly

    Partly

    Property taxes on business assets and some business taxes count. Federal income tax and self-employment tax never do, and sales tax you collect from customers and pass on isn't yours to write off.

    The rule

    Limits
    Sales tax you pay on property bought for the business is added to its cost. Sales tax imposed on your customers that you collect and pay over isn't deductible.
    Records
    Tax bills, returns and proof of payment.

    Things that usually don't count

    7

    Commuting

    Usually not

    Usually not

    Getting from home to your regular workplace is personal, however far it is. But if your home office is your main place of business, trips from there to clients or job sites can count.

    For example: A barber's daily drive to his shop doesn't count. A consultant who works from a home office and drives to a client's office can count that trip.

    What to keep as proof

    • If you claim trips, a mileage log showing they weren't commutes

    Common mistake

    Thinking a long commute, or taking work calls on the way, makes it business. It doesn't.

    For your accountant
    Technical answer
    Not deductible
    The rule
    Getting between your home and your regular place of work is commuting, a personal expense, however far it is and even if you work during the trip. That includes parking at your regular workplace.
    Limits
    0%. Exceptions: trips from a home office that is your principal place of business to other work sites, between two workplaces, and to a temporary work location can be business miles.
    Records
    If you claim trips as business, a mileage log showing they weren't commutes.
    Who it applies to
    All taxpayers.

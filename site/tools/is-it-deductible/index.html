A

Home & office

6 expenses
A-1

Home office

You can deduct part of your home costs if a specific area is used regularly and exclusively for your business and is your principal place of business (or where you meet clients).

Depends

It depends. Whether and how you deduct it depends on how and why you use it. Read the rule below.

The rule

You can deduct part of your home costs if a specific area is used regularly and exclusively for your business and is your principal place of business (or where you meet clients). Use the simplified method ($5 per square foot) or the regular method (your business-use percentage of actual home costs, on Form 8829).

Limits

Simplified method: $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet, so $1,500 at most. Either method can't exceed the gross income from the business use of your home minus your other business expenses; under the regular method the excess carries over to the next year, under the simplified method it doesn't.

Records to keep
  • Measurements or a floor plan of the office and the whole home, photos showing exclusive use, and the bills, receipts and canceled checks for every home expense you deduct (and your home's basis if you claim depreciation)
Who it applies to

Self-employed people. W-2 employees can't claim a home office deduction.

Official sources
  1. IRS Publication 587 (2025), Business Use of Your Home
  2. IRS: Simplified option for home office deduction

A-2

Office rent

Rent you pay for office, retail or other business space is deductible in the year it applies to.

Deductible100%

100% deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Rent you pay for office, retail or other business space is deductible in the year it applies to. Rent for vehicles, machinery and equipment is deductible too, for the business share of use.

Limits

No dollar limit. For rented vehicles, machinery and equipment, only the business share of use counts.

Records to keep
  • The signed lease, monthly invoices or statements, and proof of payment
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
  2. 26 CFR § 1.162-1, Business expenses

A-3

Coworking space or shared desk

A coworking membership, desk rental or meeting-room booking used for your business is rent for business space and is deductible.

Deductible100%

100% deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

A coworking membership, desk rental or meeting-room booking used for your business is rent for business space and is deductible.

Limits

Only the business share counts if part of the membership is for personal use (for example, a gym or social perks bundled in).

Records to keep
  • Membership agreement, monthly invoices and proof of payment
  • A short note of what the space is used for helps
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
  2. 26 CFR § 1.162-1, Business expenses
A-4

Utilities (electric, gas, water)

Utilities for a business location (an office, shop or studio) are deductible.

Depends

It depends. Whether and how you deduct it depends on how and why you use it. Read the rule below.

The rule

Utilities for a business location (an office, shop or studio) are deductible. Utilities for your home are deductible only through a home office deduction, as part of your business-use percentage under the regular method.

Limits

No dollar limit for a business location. At home, only the business-use share counts, and the home office income limit applies.

Records to keep
  • Utility bills and proof of payment; for a home office, your square-footage calculation
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
  2. IRS Publication 587 (2025), Business Use of Your Home

A-5

Office supplies and postage

Office supplies and postage used in your business are deductible.

Deductible100%

100% deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Office supplies and postage used in your business are deductible. Materials and supplies are generally deducted as you use them, and items you normally use up within a year can be deducted when bought.

Limits

Items that last substantially longer than a year are generally depreciated instead (or expensed under the de minimis safe harbor or Section 179).

Records to keep
  • Receipts or invoices showing what you bought and when
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
A-6

Work clothes and uniforms

Clothing is deductible only if your work requires it and it isn't suitable for ordinary street wear, such as protective gear or a uniform you wouldn't wear outside work.

Depends

It depends. Whether and how you deduct it depends on how and why you use it. Read the rule below.

The rule

Clothing is deductible only if your work requires it and it isn't suitable for ordinary street wear, such as protective gear or a uniform you wouldn't wear outside work. Everyday clothes are personal, even if you only wear them for work.

Limits

No dollar limit. The test is objective: if the item could be worn as ordinary street clothing, it doesn't qualify, however you actually use it.

Records to keep
  • Receipts
  • A note of why the item is required for your work and not suitable for everyday wear (photos help)
Who it applies to

Self-employed people.

Official sources

  2. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
  3. 26 U.S.C. § 162, Trade or business expenses

Vehicle & travel

5 expenses
B-1

Car mileage (standard rate)

Deduct your business miles times the IRS standard mileage rate instead of tracking actual car costs.

Partly

Partly deductible. Only part of the cost counts: a percentage, the business share, or up to a limit.

The rule

Deduct your business miles times the IRS standard mileage rate instead of tracking actual car costs. Business miles are trips to clients, between work locations and for business errands, never the commute.

Limits

2026 business rate: 72.5 cents a mile for Jan. 1 to June 30 and 76 cents a mile for July 1 to Dec. 31 (2025: 70 cents). You must choose the standard rate in the first year you use a car you own for business; with five or more cars used at the same time you can't use it. Business parking and tolls are deductible on top.

Records to keep
  • A mileage log kept as you go: date, destination, business purpose and miles for each trip
  • Total miles for the year and odometer readings
Who it applies to

Self-employed people who own or lease the car. W-2 employees generally can't deduct unreimbursed car costs.

Official sources
  1. IRS: Standard mileage rates
  2. IRS Topic no. 510, Business use of car
  3. IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses
Related MFA tools

B-2

Car expenses (actual costs)

Instead of the standard rate, you can deduct the business share of what the car actually costs: gas, oil, repairs, tires, insurance, registration, licenses and depreciation or lease payments.

Partly

Partly deductible. Only part of the cost counts: a percentage, the business share, or up to a limit.

The rule

Instead of the standard rate, you can deduct the business share of what the car actually costs: gas, oil, repairs, tires, insurance, registration, licenses and depreciation or lease payments. If you also drive it personally, only the business-use percentage counts.

Limits

Business-use percentage = business miles divided by total miles. Depreciation is subject to the yearly limits for passenger cars; a heavy SUV's Section 179 deduction is capped at $32,000 for tax years beginning in 2026 ($31,300 in 2025).

Records to keep
  • Every receipt for car costs, the purchase or lease papers, and a mileage log showing business and total miles
Who it applies to

Self-employed people. W-2 employees generally can't deduct unreimbursed car costs.

Official sources
  1. IRS Topic no. 510, Business use of car
  2. IRS Publication 946 (2025), How To Depreciate Property
  3. IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses

B-3

Parking and tolls

Parking fees and tolls for business trips are deductible on top of either the standard mileage rate or actual expenses.

Deductible100%

100% deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Parking fees and tolls for business trips are deductible on top of either the standard mileage rate or actual expenses.

Limits

Parking at your own regular place of business is a commuting cost and isn't deductible.

Records to keep
  • Receipts or toll statements, matched to trips in your mileage log
Who it applies to

Self-employed people; employees only in limited cases.

B-4

Business travel (flights, hotels)

Ordinary and necessary costs of traveling away from your tax home overnight for business are deductible: transportation, lodging, taxis or rideshare at the destination, baggage, laundry and business calls.

Deductible

Deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Ordinary and necessary costs of traveling away from your tax home overnight for business are deductible: transportation, lodging, taxis or rideshare at the destination, baggage, laundry and business calls. Meals on the trip are 50% deductible.

Limits

The trip has to take you away from your tax home (the general area of your main place of business) longer than a normal workday, needing sleep or rest. A trip that is mainly personal is a personal expense.

Records to keep
  • Receipts for lodging and anything $75 or more
  • The dates, destination and business purpose of the trip
Who it applies to

Self-employed people. W-2 employees generally can't deduct unreimbursed travel.

Official sources
  1. IRS Topic no. 511, Business travel expenses
  2. IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses
  3. 26 CFR § 1.274-5, Substantiation requirements
B-5

Meals while traveling

Meals while you're traveling away from home on business are generally 50% deductible.

Partly50%

50% deductible. Only part of the cost counts: a percentage, the business share, or up to a limit.

The rule

Meals while you're traveling away from home on business are generally 50% deductible. You can deduct actual costs or use the standard meal allowance for the area instead of keeping meal receipts.

Limits

50% of the cost (80% for workers subject to Department of Transportation hours-of-service limits, such as long-haul truck drivers).

Records to keep
  • Receipts if you deduct actual costs; with the standard meal allowance, records of the time, place and business purpose of the trip
Who it applies to

Self-employed people traveling overnight for business.

Official sources
  1. IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses
  2. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
  3. 26 U.S.C. § 274, Disallowance of certain entertainment, etc., expenses

Meals & entertainment

4 expenses
C-1

Business meals

A meal with a client, customer or business contact is generally 50% deductible if it isn't lavish, you (or an employee) are there, and it has a business purpose.

Partly50%

50% deductible. Only part of the cost counts: a percentage, the business share, or up to a limit.

The rule

A meal with a client, customer or business contact is generally 50% deductible if it isn't lavish, you (or an employee) are there, and it has a business purpose.

Limits

50% of the cost, including tax and tip. Food and drinks at an entertainment event count only if bought separately or listed separately on the bill.

Records to keep
  • Receipt (required for $75 or more)
  • A note of the date, place, who attended, their business relationship to you and what you discussed
  • Write it down at or near the time
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses
  2. 26 U.S.C. § 274, Disallowance of certain entertainment, etc., expenses
  3. 26 CFR § 1.274-5, Substantiation requirements
C-2

Entertainment (tickets, golf, shows)

Entertainment is not deductible, even with a clear business purpose.

Not deductible0%

Not deductible. Generally not deductible as a business expense.

The rule

Entertainment is not deductible, even with a clear business purpose. That covers tickets to games and shows, golf, and similar outings with clients or prospects.

Limits

0%. Food and drinks bought separately at an entertainment event, or listed separately on the bill, can still be a 50% business meal.

Records to keep
  • Keep records anyway if a separately billed meal was involved
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses
  2. 26 U.S.C. § 274, Disallowance of certain entertainment, etc., expenses
C-3

Staff parties and team events

Recreational and social events mainly for your employees, such as a holiday party or summer picnic, are an exception to the entertainment and 50% meal limits.

Deductible100%

100% deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Recreational and social events mainly for your employees, such as a holiday party or summer picnic, are an exception to the entertainment and 50% meal limits.

Limits

100%, as long as the event is mainly for employees in general rather than for highly compensated employees only.

Records to keep
  • Invoices and receipts, the date, and a guest list showing it was for staff
Who it applies to

Businesses with employees.

Official sources
  1. IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses
  2. 26 U.S.C. § 274, Disallowance of certain entertainment, etc., expenses
C-4

Snacks and meals for employees at work

Starting in 2026, an employer can no longer deduct the cost of food and drinks provided to employees through an on-site eating facility, as de minimis fringe benefits, or for the employer's convenience.

Not deductible0%

Not deductible. Generally not deductible as a business expense.

The rule

Starting in 2026, an employer can no longer deduct the cost of food and drinks provided to employees through an on-site eating facility, as de minimis fringe benefits, or for the employer's convenience. There are narrow exceptions in the law.

Limits

0% for amounts paid or incurred after Dec. 31, 2025. Staff parties and other recreational events mainly for employees follow a different rule.

Records to keep
  • Invoices
  • What the food was for, so the right rule can be applied
Who it applies to

Businesses with employees.

Official sources
  1. IRS Publication 15-B (2026), Employer's Tax Guide to Fringe Benefits
  2. 26 U.S.C. § 274, Disallowance of certain entertainment, etc., expenses

Tech & software

4 expenses
D-1

Cell phone and phone service

The business share of your cell phone plan is deductible.

Partly

Partly deductible. Only part of the cost counts: a percentage, the business share, or up to a limit.

The rule

The business share of your cell phone plan is deductible. For a home landline, the basic charge for the first line into your home is personal, but business long-distance calls and a second line used only for business are deductible.

Limits

Only the business-use percentage of a mixed-use phone. The phone itself is equipment: deduct it under the de minimis safe harbor, Section 179 or depreciation.

Records to keep
  • Phone bills
  • A reasonable basis for your business-use percentage (for example, a sample month of itemized usage)
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Publication 587 (2025), Business Use of Your Home
  2. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
D-2

Internet service

Internet service for a business location is deductible.

Partly

Partly deductible. Only part of the cost counts: a percentage, the business share, or up to a limit.

The rule

Internet service for a business location is deductible. At home, only the business share counts, either as part of your home office expenses or based on your business use.

Limits

Only the business-use percentage when you also use it personally.

Records to keep
  • Monthly bills and how you worked out the business percentage
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
  2. IRS Publication 587 (2025), Business Use of Your Home
D-3

Software and subscriptions

Monthly or annual subscriptions to software and online services you use for your business are ordinary business expenses.

Deductible

Deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Monthly or annual subscriptions to software and online services you use for your business are ordinary business expenses. Software you buy outright and use for more than a year is generally depreciated, though off-the-shelf software can qualify for Section 179 expensing.

Limits

Only the business share if you also use the subscription personally.

Records to keep
  • Invoices or receipts, and the account showing it's used for the business
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
  2. 26 U.S.C. § 179, Election to expense certain depreciable business assets
  3. 26 U.S.C. § 162, Trade or business expenses
D-4

Laptop or computer

A computer used in your business is a business asset.

Depends

It depends. Whether and how you deduct it depends on how and why you use it. Read the rule below.

The rule

A computer used in your business is a business asset. You can deduct it all at once using the de minimis safe harbor (if $2,500 or less per item or invoice), Section 179 or 100% bonus depreciation, or depreciate it over several years. If you also use it personally, only the business share counts.

Limits

Since 2018 computers are no longer "listed property," but personal use still reduces the deduction. If business use later falls so it's no longer mainly for business, part of a Section 179 deduction can be recaptured.

Records to keep
  • The receipt or invoice, the date you started using it for business, and a record of business versus personal use
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Publication 946 (2025), How To Depreciate Property
  2. 26 U.S.C. § 280F, Limitation on depreciation for luxury automobiles; listed property
  3. IRS: Tangible property final regulations (FAQs)
  4. 26 U.S.C. § 179, Election to expense certain depreciable business assets

Equipment & depreciation

5 expenses
E-1

Equipment and machinery (Section 179)

Section 179 lets you deduct the cost of qualifying business equipment, furniture and off-the-shelf software in the year you start using it, instead of depreciating it over several years.

Deductible

Deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Section 179 lets you deduct the cost of qualifying business equipment, furniture and off-the-shelf software in the year you start using it, instead of depreciating it over several years.

Limits

Tax years beginning in 2026: up to $2,560,000, reduced dollar for dollar once qualifying purchases exceed $4,090,000 (2025: $2,500,000 and $4,000,000). Heavy SUVs are capped at $32,000. The deduction can't exceed your taxable income from active business; the excess carries forward.

Records to keep
  • Invoices, the placed-in-service date, business-use percentage, and Form 4562
Who it applies to

Businesses of any type that buy qualifying property for active business use.

Official sources
  1. IRS Publication 946 (2025), How To Depreciate Property
  2. IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32: 2026 inflation-adjusted figures
  3. 26 U.S.C. § 179, Election to expense certain depreciable business assets
E-2

Bonus depreciation

Qualified property acquired and placed in service after Jan.

Deductible100%

100% deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Qualified property acquired and placed in service after Jan. 19, 2025 can be written off 100% in the first year through the special depreciation allowance, now permanent under the 2025 tax law (P.L. 119-21). You can elect out, or elect a smaller 40% allowance for the first tax year ending after Jan. 19, 2025.

Limits

100% for property acquired after Jan. 19, 2025. Property acquired before Jan. 20, 2025 and placed in service in 2025 is limited to 40% (60% for certain long-production property and aircraft).

Records to keep
  • Purchase contracts showing the acquisition date, invoices, placed-in-service date and Form 4562
Who it applies to

Businesses of any type.

Official sources
  1. IRS IR-2026-06: Guidance on the 100% additional first-year depreciation deduction (Notice 2026-11)
  2. IRS Publication 946 (2025), How To Depreciate Property
E-3

Small equipment purchases ($2,500 or less)

With the de minimis safe harbor election you can deduct, rather than depreciate, tangible items that cost $2,500 or less per invoice or item.

Deductible

Deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

With the de minimis safe harbor election you can deduct, rather than depreciate, tangible items that cost $2,500 or less per invoice or item. You make the election each year with a statement attached to your timely filed return.

Limits

$2,500 per invoice or item ($5,000 if you have an applicable financial statement). Items above the limit are depreciated or expensed under Section 179 or bonus depreciation.

Records to keep
  • Invoices and the election statement filed with your return
Who it applies to

Businesses of any type, including sole proprietors.

Official sources
  1. IRS: Tangible property final regulations (FAQs)
  2. IRS Publication 946 (2025), How To Depreciate Property
E-4

Tools and small equipment

Tools and professional instruments you normally use up within a year can be deducted when bought.

Deductible

Deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Tools and professional instruments you normally use up within a year can be deducted when bought. Longer-lasting tools are depreciated, unless you expense them under the de minimis safe harbor or Section 179.

Limits

Only the business share if you also use them personally.

Records to keep
  • Receipts or invoices and, for larger items, the date you started using them
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)
  2. IRS: Tangible property final regulations (FAQs)
E-5

Repairs vs. improvements

Incidental repairs and routine maintenance that keep property in normal working order are deductible.

Depends

It depends. Whether and how you deduct it depends on how and why you use it. Read the rule below.

The rule

Incidental repairs and routine maintenance that keep property in normal working order are deductible. Work that betters, restores or adapts the property to a new use is an improvement: it's capitalized and depreciated.

Limits

Small taxpayer safe harbor: buildings with an unadjusted basis of $1 million or less, if your average gross receipts are $10 million or less, can deduct repairs, maintenance and improvements up to the lesser of $10,000 or 2% of the building's basis.

Records to keep
  • Contractor invoices describing the work done, before-and-after photos, and any safe harbor election statements
Who it applies to

Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C); the same rule generally applies to partnerships and corporations.

Official sources
  1. IRS: Tangible property final regulations (FAQs)
  2. IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040) (2025)

Contractor and freelancer payments

Payments to independent contractors for work done for your business are deductible as contract labor.

Deductible100%

Payments to independent contractors for work done for your business are deductible as contract labor.

Records to keep

F-2

Employee wages

Reasonable wages, salaries and bonuses you pay employees for work actually done are deductible.

Deductible100%

100% deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Reasonable wages, salaries and bonuses you pay employees for work actually done are deductible. As a sole proprietor, what you pay yourself (owner's draws) is not a deduction.

Limits

Pay must be reasonable for the services. Reduce the deduction by certain employment credits you claim, such as the work opportunity credit.

Records to keep
  • Payroll records, Forms W-2 and W-4, timesheets and payroll tax filings
  • Keep employment tax records at least 4 years
Who it applies to

Businesses with employees.

F-3

Employer payroll taxes

The employer's share of Social Security and Medicare taxes, federal unemployment tax (FUTA) and state unemployment contributions are deductible business taxes.

Deductible100%

100% deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

The employer's share of Social Security and Medicare taxes, federal unemployment tax (FUTA) and state unemployment contributions are deductible business taxes.

Limits

Only your share as the employer. Taxes you withhold from employees' pay are part of their wages, not a separate deduction.

Records to keep
  • Forms 941 and 940, state payroll filings and deposit confirmations
Who it applies to

Businesses with employees.

F-4

Employee benefit programs

Contributions to employee benefit programs, such as accident and health plans, group-term life insurance and dependent care assistance, are deductible.

Deductible

Deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

Contributions to employee benefit programs, such as accident and health plans, group-term life insurance and dependent care assistance, are deductible.

Limits

Reduce the deduction by any small employer health insurance credit. Benefits for yourself as a self-employed person don't go here. Employer-paid education assistance is excludable for employees up to $5,250 a year.

Records to keep
  • Plan documents, invoices and payment records, and who was covered
Who it applies to

Businesses with employees.

F-5

Employee awards and holiday gifts

Tangible awards for length of service or safety, given as part of a meaningful presentation, are deductible within limits.

Deductible

Deductible. Generally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.

The rule

A sole proprietor's charitable donations aren't a business expense either, though they may be deductible on Schedule A if you itemize.

Keep receipts, canceled checks and other documents that support each deduction. For travel, meals, gifts and car use, also record the amount, date, place, business purpose and business relationship, and keep receipts for lodging and anything $75 or more. Keep records at least until the period to assess tax runs out, generally 3 years after you file, and employment tax records for at least 4 years.

You often have a choice. Items of $2,500 or less per invoice or item can be deducted under the de minimis safe harbor election. Larger purchases can be expensed under Section 179 (up to $2,560,000 for tax years beginning in 2026) or with 100% bonus depreciation for property acquired after Jan. 19, 2025, or depreciated over several years. The best choice depends on your income this year and in later years, so it's worth running the numbers.

It's written for self-employed people and small businesses. W-2 employees generally can't deduct unreimbursed job costs such as car expenses or a home office.

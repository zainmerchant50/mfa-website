How to read the answers

Each expense gets one of four labels. Open any card for the rule in plain English, the limits, the records to keep and the official source it comes from.

  • DeductibleGenerally deductible as a business expense, if it's ordinary and necessary for your business.
  • PartlyOnly part of the cost counts: a percentage, the business share, or up to a limit.
  • DependsWhether and how you deduct it depends on how and why you use it. Read the rule below.
  • Not deductibleGenerally not deductible as a business expense.

Answers are written for sole proprietors and single-member LLCs filing Schedule C, and most apply the same way to partnerships and corporations. Figures are for the 2026 tax year where the IRS has published them. The general test is that the cost is ordinary and necessary for carrying on your trade or business. Personal, living and family costs aren't deductible, and when something is used for both business and personal purposes, only the business share counts. Some expenses also have their own limits, such as the 50% limit on business meals and the $25 limit on gifts.

Holiday parties and similar events mainly for employees are fully deductible. Starting in 2026, food and drinks an employer provides to employees at work, such as office snacks or on-site meals for the employer's convenience, are no longer deductible.

Keep receipts, canceled checks and other documents that support each deduction. For travel, meals, gifts and car use, also record the amount, date, place, business purpose and business relationship, and keep receipts for lodging and anything $75 or more. Keep records at least until the period to assess tax runs out, generally 3 years after you file, and employment tax records for at least 4 years.

The best choice depends on your income this year and in later years, so it's worth running the numbers.

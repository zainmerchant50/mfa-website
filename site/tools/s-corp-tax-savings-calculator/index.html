What this compares

An LLC owner who is taxed as a sole proprietor pays self-employment tax (15.3% on 92.35% of profit) on all of the profit. An S corporation owner who works in the business is paid a salary and the company pays payroll taxes on that salary only (the same 15.3%, split between employee and employer, on the full salary). Profit left over as distributions is not subject to payroll or self-employment tax. The savings come from that difference, minus the extra costs of running a corporation.

Not included: federal and state income tax differences, the qualified business income (QBI) deduction, and the deduction effects of half of SE tax versus the employer payroll tax. Those can move the answer in either direction, so use this as a first filter, not a final answer.

What else to weigh

  • Reasonable compensation. The IRS expects an owner-employee to be paid a reasonable salary before taking distributions. A very low salary is a common audit trigger. Base it on what the role would cost to hire.
  • Form 2553 timing. The S corporation election is made on Form 2553. For 2027, calendar-year entities have until March 15, 2027; check the 2027 deadlines for the date for your entity.
  • Payroll obligations. You must run payroll, make payroll tax deposits and file quarterly and year-end payroll returns, and the state may add its own payroll registrations.
  • Extra filings and fees. A separate corporate return (Form 1120-S), K-1s, and state-level fees or taxes for S corporations.
  • Retirement, health insurance and loans. Salary affects retirement contribution limits, and owner health insurance has its own S corporation rules.
  • Formation first. You need a corporation or an LLC before electing S status. See our LLC formation service for NJ and NY.
